# Ionisation in the cell
id: cell
summary: What happens in a cell after an X-ray photon ionises it.
areas: Ionisation in cells
diagram: cell-damage
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s5–7

Ionisation takes place within materials through which a beam of ionising radiation passes. The result is the ejection of an electron from an atom and the formation of an **ion pair**. What effect does this have on the body tissues of an irradiated person?

Ionisation breaks chemical bonds. This produces free, highly reactive ions, capable of forming new bonds with other ions. Some of the ions formed will **recombine**, within about 10⁻¹¹ s. If recombination produces the same atoms and molecules as before irradiation, then **no harmful effect** has taken place. However, the bonding of free ions to other ions can produce **new molecules** within the cells of the tissue.

Cells are mostly water, so most of the damage from X-rays is **indirect**: ionised water forms **free radicals**, which go on to damage DNA. Less often, the radiation ionises the DNA itself (**direct action**).

If the original molecule was important to the normal functioning of the cell, then the cell may behave abnormally as a result: it may **repair** the damage, **die**, or survive with a **mutation**. Biological damage has taken place. Such damage is categorised as having **stochastic** effects or **deterministic** effects.

- * By free radicals formed from ionised water
- By the cell heating up
> Cells are mostly water. Most damage from X-rays is indirect: ionised water forms free radicals, which go on to damage the DNA.

## Key points
- Ionisation ejects an electron and forms an ion pair, breaking chemical bonds.
- If the ions recombine into the same molecules as before, no harmful effect has taken place.
- Free radicals formed from water cause most DNA damage (indirect action).
- A damaged cell may repair itself, die, or survive with a mutation.

# Stochastic and deterministic effects
id: stochastic-deterministic
summary: The difference between effects that may occur and effects that will.
areas: Stochastic effects; Deterministic effects
diagram: dose-response
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s8–9, s17–19

Biological damage is categorised as having stochastic effects or deterministic (non-stochastic) effects.

The probability of a **stochastic** effect is governed by the laws of **chance**. The greater the dose received, the greater the chance of the effect occurring, but the **severity** of the effect is unrelated to the dose received. This leads to an important concept: there is **no safe dose limit**. All radiation doses carry some risk of having an effect. The main stochastic effects are radiation-induced **cancers** and genetic effects.

A **deterministic** effect is one for which there is usually a **threshold dose**, below which the effect does not occur. Above it, the **severity** of the effect increases with the radiation dose. Skin reddening (erythema), hair loss and cataracts are examples.

We can say that a stochastic effect **may** occur. A deterministic effect **will** occur above a particular dose.

The doses required for deterministic effects tend to be very large. For a single whole-body dose:

- about 1 Gy: a temporary depression of the blood count, which is recoverable;
- about 5 Gy: bone marrow failure;
- about 10 Gy: damage to the lining of the gastrointestinal tract;
- about 20 Gy: damage to the central nervous system.

When are we likely to meet each in radiography? Every examination involving ionising radiation carries some risk of a **stochastic** effect. Deterministic effects are dose specific and more likely in **radiotherapy**, where skin reddening and hair loss may be seen. Diagnostic doses are far below the thresholds.

## Key points
- Stochastic: governed by chance. The greater the dose, the greater the chance of the effect; the severity is unrelated to dose. There is no safe dose limit. Example: cancer.
- Deterministic: a threshold dose, below which the effect does not occur; the severity increases with dose. Examples: erythema, hair loss, cataract.
- A stochastic effect may occur; a deterministic effect will occur above a particular dose.
- Diagnostic doses are below deterministic thresholds: the concern is stochastic risk.

# Somatic and genetic effects
id: somatic-genetic
summary: Effects on the individual irradiated, and effects passed to future generations.
areas: Genetic and somatic effects
diagram: sorter
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s14–16, s20

The division of risk into stochastic and deterministic effects is relatively recent. Formerly, effects were referred to as somatic and genetic effects.

A **somatic** effect is an effect upon the **individual** irradiated. It includes both the stochastic and the deterministic effects to that individual.

**Genetic** effects may be caused by damage to the chromosomes in the **ova and spermatozoa**. If reproduction is still possible, the genetic structure of the offspring will have changed, and the change could be passed down to future generations. Genetic effects are stochastic.

The radiation dose of interest here is the dose to the reproductive organs of individuals who are of reproductive capacity. To minimise these effects, the **gonad dose** must be reduced as far as possible, using good technique and appropriate equipment.

Severe effects tend to be eliminated, by a short life span or the inability to reproduce, so it is the less severe effects we are guarding against. Some reports from the Hiroshima data do not support the concept that genetic effects are problematic. However, such protection is required in law.

## Key points
- Somatic effects are effects upon the individual; they include both stochastic and deterministic effects.
- Genetic effects are caused by damage to the chromosomes in the ova and spermatozoa, and could be passed to future generations. They are stochastic.
- Reduce the gonad dose as far as possible for individuals of reproductive capacity.

# Counting the risk
id: population-risk
summary: How stochastic risk is estimated, and what it means for a population.
areas: Stochastic effects
diagram: population-risk
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s10–12

For radiation-induced cancers, we compare the number of cancers produced in an irradiated sample population with the number in an unirradiated sample. From this we can calculate a **risk factor** for that cancer for a particular radiation exposure. Knowing the risk, we can define an acceptable level of risk, and hence calculate maximum permissible doses.

For the whole body, the usual estimate is about **5% per sievert** for a fatal cancer. A chest X-ray (about 0.02 mSv) carries a risk of about **1 in a million**.

The lecture's example: the risk factor for radiation-induced leukaemia is estimated to be 2 × 10⁻³ per Sv. If we gave a population of 1 million the maximum permissible annual occupational exposure (20 mSv), how many would we expect to develop radiation-induced leukaemia?

10⁶ × (2 × 10⁻³) × (20 × 10⁻³) = **40**, or 1 in 25,000.

This seems high, but bear in mind that 20 mSv equates to about 1,000 chest X-rays. (The slide says 400, using an older chest dose.)

The risk applies to a **population**. Nobody can say in advance **who** will be affected, which is what makes the effect stochastic. As these cancers are stochastic effects, minimising the radiation dose minimises the chance of their occurring.

# Radiosensitivity and tissue weighting
id: radiosensitivity
summary: Why some tissues are more radiosensitive, and how effective dose allows for it.
areas: Radiosensitivity
diagram: tissue-weights
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s13, s27

Some tissues are more **radiosensitive** than others, particularly those whose cells are **rapidly dividing**. Bone marrow, the lining of the gut and the gonads are examples. Risk factors therefore have to be adjusted according to the area irradiated.

Each organ is allocated a **tissue weighting factor**, depending on the susceptibility of that tissue to undergo stochastic change. It is defined as the risk of stochastic effects being induced in that organ when it is irradiated singly, compared with the total risk when the same dose is received by the whole body.

In ICRP 103, red bone marrow, lung, stomach, colon and breast each have 0.12; the gonads 0.08; the thyroid, liver, oesophagus and bladder 0.04; skin, brain, bone surface and salivary glands 0.01.

**Effective dose** adds up the dose to each organ multiplied by its weighting factor. This is why an examination of the chest or abdomen gives a far higher effective dose than one of the hand or knee, even at the same exposure.

- * The lungs
- They are the same
> In ICRP 103 the lungs have 0.12 and the skin 0.01: the lungs count twelve times as much towards effective dose.

## Key points
- Tissues whose cells are rapidly dividing are the most radiosensitive.
- Each organ is allocated a weighting factor, depending on its susceptibility to stochastic change.
- Effective dose = the sum of each organ's dose × its weighting factor.

# Risk versus benefit
id: risk-benefit
summary: Justification, and keeping the dose as low as reasonably practicable.
areas: Risk and benefit
diagram: justify
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s21–26

Risk versus benefit is a very important concept. It governs most of our practice, and it is the core of the legislation used to enforce good practice.

There is a risk, however small, associated with every examination using ionising radiation. How then can we justify its use? There must be a **benefit** gained by the patient, and each examination must be **clinically justified**. This is the risk–benefit relationship: no radiological procedure should be undertaken unless there is sound clinical justification to demonstrate a likely benefit to the patient.

To help the benefit outweigh the risk, we minimise the dose administered. Stochastic effects can occur with any dose, but the greater the dose, the greater the chance of an effect. This leads to the ALARA/ALARP principle. For any examination, the dose administered should be:

- **As Low As Reasonably Achievable** (ALARA): the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP);
- **As Low As Reasonably Practicable** (ALARP): the Ionising Radiations Regulations (IRR).

Good technique, the most appropriate equipment for the examination, updated equipment and updated training for staff all minimise the dose. But is the cost involved reasonable? That is the meaning of "reasonably".

# What can we do?
id: what-can-we-do
summary: Who needs protecting, and the factors that affect the dose given.
areas: Radiation protection; Risk and benefit
diagram: dose-choices
status: draft
sources: 9-1 s2–4, s28–37

The purpose of radiation protection is to **minimise the risk** of receiving a dose. Medical exposure is both significant and **controllable**, and it is governed by regulations to ensure the dose is minimised.

Who needs protecting? **Patients**, and also anyone else who may receive a dose: someone holding a patient, others who may need to be in the room, and not least the **person performing the examination**.

Several factors affect the dose given. What follows is an outline, not an exhaustive list.

- **Room layout.** Its primary effect is to provide adequate protection for staff and others. Walls and doors need to be radiation proof, with a radiation barrier for the staff involved in the examination. The protection given by any barrier is stated as its **lead equivalence**: the thickness of lead that would absorb the same amount of radiation.
- **Equipment under the radiographer's control.** Choose equipment appropriate to the examination. Automatic exposure devices, secondary radiation grids and digital techniques should be used appropriately, as should adequate collimation.
- **Equipment the radiographer cannot alter.** Filtration, rectification and tube shielding all affect the dose. This shows the importance of selecting the equipment most appropriate to the examination.
- **The operator.** A diagnostic image requires sufficient exposure, and therefore dose, to produce it. The decision on which images are required is based on the clinical details provided.
- **The patient.** A large patient may require an increase in exposure factors, compared with a small or average patient, to produce an image of the same quality, and consequently receives an increased dose. An uncooperative patient needs more care to avoid repeats.
- **Imaging protocols.** Departmental imaging protocols for each examination are a legal requirement. The RCR's iRefer guidelines suggest protocols for referring clinicians; they are a guide, varied in each department by local protocol.
