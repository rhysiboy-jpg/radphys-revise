# The regulations
id: regulations
summary: IR(ME)R 2017 and IRR17: who each protects and who enforces them.
areas: Legislation
diagram: regulations
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s22–23; Medico-legal s4, s13–18, s33–34

Radiographers have a legal obligation to protect patients, themselves and others from unnecessary radiation exposure. This obligation is governed by a framework of laws, regulations and guidelines, aimed at minimising radiation risks while maximising the benefits of radiographic procedures.

The **Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations 2017**, **IR(ME)R 2017**, are for the **protection of patients**. They set out requirements for the justification and optimisation of medical exposures, and the responsibilities of the duty holders. They are enforced by **Healthcare Inspectorate Wales (HIW)** in Wales and the **Care Quality Commission (CQC)** in England.

The **Ionising Radiations Regulations 2017**, **IRR17**, are for the **protection of staff and members of the public**. They cover dose limits, designated areas, local rules and the roles of the radiation protection adviser and supervisor. They are enforced by the **Health and Safety Executive (HSE)**.

Other legislation applies too:

- The **Health and Safety at Work Act 1974** imposes general duties on employers to ensure the health, safety and welfare of their employees, including protection from radiation hazards in the workplace.
- The **Data Protection Act 2018** (with UK GDPR) protects patients' and staff data.
- The **Human Rights Act 1998** sets out the fundamental rights and freedoms that everyone is entitled to.

**Confidentiality** is a crucial aspect of the radiographer's duty of care. Radiographers are entrusted with sensitive patient information, and must ensure that it is kept confidential and disclosed only to authorised individuals for legitimate purposes. A breach of patient confidentiality is a breach of the Data Protection Act 2018 and of the duty of confidentiality owed to patients.

## Try this
- Sort each situation: is it about **patients**, **workers and the public**, or **personal information**?
- Check who enforces each: HIW or the CQC, or the HSE.

## Key points
- IR(ME)R 2017 is for the protection of patients; enforced by HIW in Wales and the CQC in England.
- IRR17 is for the protection of staff and the public; enforced by the HSE.
- Patient information is protected by the Data Protection Act 2018 and the duty of confidentiality.

# IR(ME)R duty holders
id: duty-holders
summary: The employer, referrer, practitioner and operator, and what each is responsible for.
areas: IR(ME)R duty holders
diagram: duty-holders
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s18–22; 12-2 s24, s45

IR(ME)R 2017 sets out the responsibilities of four **duty holders**.

- The **employer** provides the framework: written **procedures and protocols**, the **entitlement** of the other duty holders, **diagnostic reference levels** and training.
- The **referrer**, a registered healthcare professional, refers the patient and must supply sufficient **clinical information** for the exposure to be justified.
- The **practitioner** **justifies** the exposure: decides that the benefit outweighs the risk.
- The **operator** carries out any **practical aspect** of the exposure, such as positioning the patient, selecting the exposure factors or making the exposure. An operator can **authorise** an exposure by following the practitioner's written authorisation guidelines.

One person can hold more than one role: a radiographer is often both practitioner and operator.

Radiographers play a critical role in radiation protection. They are responsible for ensuring that appropriate techniques and protocols are used to minimise radiation exposure while obtaining diagnostically useful images:

- **Collimation**: proper collimation of the X-ray beam restricts the radiation field to the area of clinical interest, reducing unnecessary exposure to adjacent tissues.
- **Optimisation**: exposure factors are selected for the patient's size, the anatomical region and the clinical indication, to achieve diagnostic image quality with the lowest possible radiation dose.

## Key points
- Employer: procedures, protocols, entitlement, DRLs and training.
- Referrer: refers the patient with sufficient clinical information.
- Practitioner: justifies the exposure.
- Operator: carries out the practical aspects; can authorise under the practitioner's guidelines.

# Justification and pregnancy
id: justification
summary: Weighing benefit against risk, and why we ask about pregnancy.
areas: Justification and optimisation
diagram: justify
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s18–19; 9-1 s23–24

No radiological procedure should be undertaken unless there is sound clinical justification to demonstrate a likely benefit to the patient. **Justification** means deciding that the expected **benefit** of an exposure outweighs its **risk**. Radiographic procedures must be justified on clinical need, which means considering the clinical question, any previous imaging, and whether an examination using **less or no ionising radiation**, such as ultrasound or MRI, would answer it.

Once an exposure is justified, it is **optimised**: the exposure factors are chosen to minimise the radiation dose without compromising image quality, keeping the dose as low as reasonably practicable.

For patients of **childbearing potential**, IR(ME)R requires the employer to have **procedures for making enquiries about pregnancy**, so that a possible fetus is considered in justification and optimisation. This matters most when the pelvis is in or near the primary beam. If pregnancy cannot be excluded, the practitioner weighs the clinical urgency before the exposure goes ahead. Your department's procedure sets out exactly how the enquiry is made and recorded.

# IRR17: limits, areas and advisers
id: irr17
summary: Dose limits, controlled and supervised areas, the RPA and RPS, and pregnant staff.
areas: IRR17
diagram: designated-areas
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s32; IRR17 Schedule 3

IRR17 sets **annual dose limits**. For employees aged 18 or over, the effective dose limit is **20 mSv** a year; for members of the public it is **1 mSv**. Workers likely to exceed **6 mSv** a year must be **classified** and monitored.

Areas are **designated**:

- A **controlled area** is one where special procedures are needed to restrict exposure, or where someone is likely to receive more than 6 mSv a year. Entry is restricted and **local rules** apply.
- A **supervised area** is one where conditions need to be kept under review, or where someone is likely to receive more than 1 mSv a year.

The employer must consult a **radiation protection adviser (RPA)**, an expert who advises on complying with IRR17. A **radiation protection supervisor (RPS)** ensures that work in an area follows the local rules.

Once an employee has told the employer **in writing** that she is **pregnant**, the employer must ensure that the dose to the **fetus** is unlikely to exceed **1 mSv** for the remainder of the pregnancy.

# Time, distance and shielding
id: time-distance-shielding
summary: The three ways staff reduce their dose, and the inverse square law.
areas: Time, distance and shielding
diagram: isl
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s44; 9-1 s29–31; Modifying Exposure

Staff reduce their own dose in three ways: time, distance and shielding.

**Time.** Dose is proportional to the time spent exposed, so keep the time near radiation to a minimum.

**Distance.** Intensity follows the **inverse square law**. The same radiation spreads over a larger area as it travels, so the intensity is proportional to 1/distance². **Double the distance** and the intensity falls to **a quarter**; treble it and it falls to a ninth.

**Shielding.** Walls, doors and radiation barriers protect staff and others. The protection given by any barrier is stated in terms of its **lead equivalence**: the thickness of lead which would absorb the same amount of radiation as the material, when exposed to radiation of the same type and quality. An apron of 0.35 mm lead equivalence absorbs as much as 0.35 mm of lead.

Current UK guidance (from the BIR, IPEM, RCR and SCoR, 2020) **no longer recommends** routinely placing lead shields on patients for plain radiography: shields can obscure anatomy, interfere with the AEC and lead to repeats, for very little benefit. (The lecture slide predates this guidance.)

- Routine contact shielding of patients is no longer recommended in the UK.

# Consent and the law
id: consent-law
summary: Informed consent, refusal, negligence and vicarious liability.
areas: Consent; Medico-legal
diagram: consent
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s6–12, s31–32

**Informed consent** is a fundamental aspect of the radiographer's duty of care. It requires the radiographer to obtain permission from the patient before any radiographic procedure, ensuring that the patient understands the nature of the procedure, its potential risks and benefits, and any available alternatives. It has four elements:

- **Disclosure**: the radiographer must provide clear and comprehensive information about the procedure, including its purpose, risks, benefits and possible alternatives.
- **Understanding**: the patient must be able to understand the information, including the implications of having or refusing the procedure.
- **Voluntariness**: consent must be given voluntarily, without coercion or undue influence.
- **Capacity**: the patient must have the capacity to make informed decisions about their healthcare.

For most plain radiography, consent is given **verbally**, or by the patient's actions after an explanation; a signed form is not needed. Consent comes from common law and professional standards, and IR(ME)R requires patients to be given information on the **benefits and risks** of the exposure.

A patient has the right to **refuse**. Respecting patient autonomy is paramount. The radiographer should address the patient's concerns, explain the necessity of the procedure, its potential benefits and the measures in place to minimise the dose, then respect the decision, inform the referrer and record what happened.

**Negligence** is a failure to exercise the reasonable care that would be expected in similar circumstances, resulting in **harm**. The **standard of care** is the level of care, skill and diligence that a reasonably competent radiographer would provide in similar circumstances. Negligence needs a duty of care, a breach of that standard, and harm caused by the breach.

**Vicarious liability** holds an **employer** responsible for the actions or omissions of its employees within the scope of their employment.

## Key points
- Informed consent: disclosure, understanding, voluntariness and capacity; usually verbal for plain radiography.
- A patient with capacity has the right to refuse: explain, respect the decision, inform the referrer and record it.
- Negligence: a failure to meet the standard of a reasonably competent radiographer, resulting in harm.
- Vicarious liability: the employer is responsible for employees' actions within the scope of their employment.
