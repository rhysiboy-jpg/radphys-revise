# The regulations
id: regulations
summary: IR(ME)R 2017 and IRR17: who each protects and who enforces them.
areas: Legislation
diagram: regulations
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s22–23; Medico-legal s4, s13–18, s33–34

Two sets of regulations govern ionising radiation in UK healthcare.

The **Ionising Radiation (Medical Exposure) Regulations 2017**, **IR(ME)R**, protect **patients** having medical exposures (and carers, comforters and research volunteers). They cover justification, optimisation, the duty holders' roles and diagnostic reference levels. They are enforced by **Healthcare Inspectorate Wales** (HIW) in Wales and the **Care Quality Commission** (CQC) in England.

The **Ionising Radiations Regulations 2017**, **IRR17**, protect **workers** and **members of the public**. They set dose limits, designated areas, local rules and the roles of the radiation protection adviser and supervisor. As health and safety law, they are enforced by the **Health and Safety Executive** (HSE).

Other law applies too. Patients' personal and health information is protected by the **Data Protection Act 2018** (with UK GDPR), alongside the professional duty of **confidentiality**.

## Try this
- Sort each situation: is it about **patients**, **workers and the public**, or **personal information**?
- Check who enforces each: HIW or the CQC, the HSE.

## Key points
- IR(ME)R 2017 protects patients; enforced by HIW in Wales and the CQC in England.
- IRR17 protects workers and the public; enforced by the HSE.
- Patient information is protected by the Data Protection Act 2018 and the duty of confidentiality.

# IR(ME)R duty holders
id: duty-holders
summary: The employer, referrer, practitioner and operator, and what each is responsible for.
areas: IR(ME)R duty holders
diagram: duty-holders
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s18–22; 12-2 s24, s45

IR(ME)R 2017 gives four **duty holders** their own responsibilities.

- The **employer** provides the framework: written **procedures and protocols**, **entitlement** of the other duty holders, **diagnostic reference levels** and training.
- The **referrer**, a registered healthcare professional, refers the patient and must supply enough **clinical information** for the exposure to be justified.
- The **practitioner** **justifies** the exposure: decides that its benefit outweighs the risk.
- The **operator** carries out any **practical aspect** of the exposure, such as positioning the patient, selecting the exposure factors or making the exposure. An operator can **authorise** an exposure by following the practitioner's written authorisation guidelines.

One person can hold more than one role: a radiographer is often both practitioner and operator.

## Try this
- Work through the steps in order and choose the duty holder for each.
- Notice how many steps belong to the operator.

## Key points
- Employer: procedures, protocols, entitlement, DRLs and training.
- Referrer: refers the patient with enough clinical information.
- Practitioner: justifies the exposure.
- Operator: carries out practical aspects; can authorise under the practitioner's guidelines.

# Justification and pregnancy
id: justification
summary: Weighing benefit against risk, and why we ask about pregnancy.
areas: Justification and optimisation
diagram: justify
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s18–19; 9-1 s23–24

**Justification** asks whether the expected **benefit** of an exposure outweighs its **risk**. That means considering the clinical question, any previous imaging, and whether a test with **less or no ionising radiation** (such as ultrasound or MRI) would answer it. Once an exposure is justified, it is **optimised**: kept as low as reasonably practicable while still answering the question.

For patients of **childbearing potential**, IR(ME)R requires the employer to have **procedures for asking about pregnancy**, so that a possible fetus is considered in justification and optimisation. This matters most when the pelvis is in or near the primary beam. If pregnancy can't be ruled out, the practitioner weighs the clinical urgency before the exposure goes ahead. Your department's procedure sets exactly how the enquiry is done and recorded.

## Key points
- Justification: the net benefit must outweigh the risk; consider previous imaging and non-ionising alternatives.
- Optimisation: once justified, keep the dose ALARP.
- Ask about pregnancy following the employer's procedure, so a fetus can be considered.

# IRR17: limits, areas and advisers
id: irr17
summary: Dose limits, controlled and supervised areas, the RPA and RPS, and pregnant staff.
areas: IRR17
diagram: designated-areas
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s32; IRR17 Schedule 3

IRR17 sets **annual dose limits**. For employees aged 18 or over, the effective dose limit is **20 mSv** a year; for members of the public, **1 mSv**. Workers likely to exceed **6 mSv** a year must be **classified** and monitored.

Areas are **designated**. A **controlled area** is one where special procedures are needed to restrict exposure, or where someone is likely to receive more than 6 mSv a year; entry is restricted and **local rules** apply. A **supervised area** is one where conditions need to be kept under review, or where someone is likely to receive more than 1 mSv a year.

The employer must consult a **radiation protection adviser** (RPA), an expert who advises on complying with IRR17. A **radiation protection supervisor** (RPS) makes sure work in an area follows the local rules.

Once an employee tells the employer **in writing** that she is **pregnant**, the employer must ensure the dose to the **fetus** is unlikely to exceed **1 mSv** for the rest of the pregnancy.

# Time, distance and shielding
id: time-distance-shielding
summary: The three ways staff keep their dose down, and the inverse square law.
areas: Time, distance and shielding
diagram: isl
status: draft
sources: 12-2 s44; 9-1 s29–31; Modifying Exposure

Staff reduce their own dose in three ways.

**Time**: dose is proportional to the time spent exposed, so keep time near radiation to a minimum.

**Distance**: intensity follows the **inverse square law**. The same radiation spreads over a larger area as it travels, so intensity is proportional to 1/distance². **Double the distance** and the intensity falls to **a quarter**; treble it and it falls to a ninth. Stepping back is one of the most effective protections there is.

**Shielding**: barriers, walls and aprons absorb radiation. Their protection is given as **lead equivalence**: the thickness of lead that would attenuate the same amount of radiation of the same type and quality. An apron of 0.35 mm lead equivalent attenuates the beam like 0.35 mm of lead.

Current UK guidance (from the BIR, IPEM, RCR and SCoR, 2020) **no longer recommends** routinely placing lead shields on patients for plain radiography: shields can hide anatomy, interfere with the AEC and lead to repeats, for very little benefit. (The lecture slide predates this guidance.)

> The inverse square law: double the distance and the same radiation spreads over four times the area.

## Key points
- Time: less time, less dose.
- Distance: inverse square law; double the distance, a quarter of the intensity.
- Shielding: barriers and aprons, rated by lead equivalence.
- Routine patient contact shielding is no longer recommended in the UK.

# Consent and the law
id: consent-law
summary: Valid consent, refusal, negligence and vicarious liability.
areas: Consent; Medico-legal
diagram: consent
status: draft
sources: Medico-legal s6–12, s31–32

Valid **informed consent** has four elements:

- **Disclosure**: clear information about the purpose, risks, benefits and alternatives.
- **Understanding**: the patient can understand and weigh that information.
- **Voluntariness**: the decision is free from pressure.
- **Capacity**: the patient is able to make the decision.

For most plain radiography, consent is given **verbally**, or by the patient's actions after an explanation; a signed form isn't needed. Consent comes from common law and professional standards; IR(ME)R requires patients to be given information on the **benefits and risks** of the exposure. If a patient with capacity **refuses** a justified examination, respect their decision: explain the benefits and risks, answer their concerns, inform the referrer and record what happened.

**Negligence** is a failure to take the care a reasonably competent radiographer would take, which **causes harm**. It needs a duty of care, a breach of the expected standard, and harm caused by that breach. Under **vicarious liability**, the **employer** can be held responsible for what employees do in the course of their work.

## Try this
- For each scenario, decide which element of valid consent is missing.
- Look closely at the refusal: is anything missing?

## Key points
- Valid consent: disclosure, understanding, voluntariness and capacity; usually verbal for plain radiography.
- A patient with capacity can refuse: explain, respect, inform the referrer and record.
- Negligence: a breach of the standard of a reasonably competent professional that causes harm.
- Vicarious liability: the employer answers for employees' actions at work.
