# Units and energy
id: units
summary: SI units, the electronvolt, and how energy changes form.
areas: Units; Energy
diagram: electron-energy
status: draft
sources: 3-1 s6–7; 3-2 s3, s15

The **Système International**, or **SI**, has seven **base units**: the metre (length), second (time), kilogram (mass), ampere (electric current), kelvin (temperature), candela (luminous intensity) and mole (amount of substance). An unlimited number of **derived units** can be made from these seven, and some have their own names and symbols: the hertz, the volt, the joule, the coulomb, the becquerel and the **gray** (Gy), the unit of absorbed dose. Prefixes give decimal multiples and sub-multiples, such as kilo (k), milli (m) and micro (µ).

**Energy** may be defined as the ability to do work. It is measured in the SI unit of the **joule** (J). Radiography also uses a non-SI unit, the **electronvolt** (eV): 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, the energy one electron gains when it is accelerated through 1 volt. A **keV** is 1,000 eV.

This links directly to the X-ray tube. An electron accelerated through **80 kV** arrives at the target with **80 keV** of energy, and that is the highest energy any X-ray photon from the tube can have.

The **law of conservation of energy** says that energy is not created or destroyed: it changes from one form to another. In the X-ray tube, electrical energy becomes the kinetic energy of the electrons. At the target about **99%** of that becomes **heat** and only about **1%** becomes X-rays.

## Predict
An electron is accelerated across 80 kV. How much energy does it gain?
- 80 eV
- * 80 keV
- 80 joules
> Each volt gives one electron 1 eV of energy, so 80,000 V gives 80,000 eV: 80 keV. This is the highest energy any X-ray photon from the tube can have.

## Try this
- Set **80 kV**: the electron arrives with 80 keV, the highest photon energy the tube can make.
- Halve the voltage: the energy halves too.
- Choose each **prefix** to see how µ, m, k and M relate the units used in radiography.

## Key points
- SI has seven base units (metre, second, kilogram, ampere, kelvin, candela, mole); all other units are derived from them.
- The gray is the derived unit of absorbed dose: 1 J/kg.
- Energy is the ability to do work, measured in joules. 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; electrons crossing an 80 kV tube gain 80 keV.
- Energy is conserved: it changes form. In the tube, about 99% becomes heat and about 1% X-rays.

# The atom and the Bohr model
id: atom
summary: Protons, neutrons and electrons, atomic number, and isotopes.
areas: Bohr model; Isotopes
diagram: bohr
status: draft
sources: 2-2 s6–13

The **atom** is the fundamental building block of matter. Atomic and subatomic theories are complicated, so radiography uses the **Bohr**, or planetary, **model**.

The **nucleus** contains **protons**, which have a positive charge, and **neutrons**, which have zero charge. **Electrons** have a negative charge. In Bohr's model the electrons orbit the nucleus at discrete energy levels, called **shells**, and are held in place by electrostatic forces. The shells are referred to as **K, L, M** and so on, outwards from the nucleus. To move an electron from one shell to another involves a transfer of energy.

The **atomic number**, **Z**, is the number of protons in the nucleus. It determines the element: every atom with 74 protons is tungsten. A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons. The **atomic mass number**, **A**, is the total number of protons and neutrons, so tungsten-184 has 184 − 74 = 110 neutrons.

**Isotopes** are variants of an element with the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 are both chlorine, as both have 17 protons: Cl-35 has 18 neutrons (35 − 17) and Cl-37 has 20 (37 − 17). Some isotopes have unstable nuclei. These are **radioisotopes**.

Which of a tungsten atom's shells holds its electrons most tightly?
- The outermost shell
- * The K shell, nearest the nucleus
- All the shells equally
> Electrons in the inner shells have a higher binding energy. Tungsten's K-shell electrons are bound by about 69.5 keV.

## Try this
- Change the element and count the electrons in each shell.
- Compare carbon (Z = 6) with tungsten (Z = 74): many more electrons, and many more shells.

## Key points
- The nucleus contains protons (positive) and neutrons (zero charge); electrons (negative) orbit in shells.
- Atomic number, Z = number of protons, which determines the element. Atomic mass number, A = protons + neutrons.
- Isotopes: the same number of protons, a different number of neutrons. Those with unstable nuclei are radioisotopes.
- Shells are referred to as K, L, M and so on, outwards from the nucleus.

# Shells and binding energy
id: shells
summary: How many electrons each shell holds, and how tightly they are held.
areas: Electron shells; Binding energy
diagram: bohr
status: draft
sources: 2-2 s6–7, s15–17

An electron shell cannot contain more than **2n²** electrons, where n is the principal quantum number of the shell. The **K** shell (n = 1) holds 2 electrons, the **L** shell (n = 2) holds 8, the **M** shell (n = 3) holds 18 and the **N** shell (n = 4) holds 32.

**Electron binding energy** is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom. The negative electron is normally attracted to the positive nucleus, and energy is needed to overcome this attraction.

Binding energy differs from shell to shell and from element to element:

- Electrons in the **inner shells** have a higher binding energy, so it is greatest in the K shell.
- A **higher atomic number** gives a higher binding energy: the more protons in the nucleus, the higher its charge.

For tungsten (Z = 74) the K-shell binding energy is about **69.5 keV**; for lead (Z = 82) it is about 88 keV, for iodine (Z = 53) about 33 keV and for oxygen (Z = 8) about 0.5 keV. The lecture's table gives rounder values for the L and M shells (tungsten L 11.5 keV, M 2.5 keV); the diagram uses reference values (L 10.2 keV, M 1.8 keV) and shows the lecture's beside them.

## Try this
- Read the K, L and M binding energies for tungsten, then switch to carbon.
- Change the element: a lead K-shell electron is held over 300 times more tightly than a carbon one.

## Key points
- A shell cannot contain more than 2n² electrons: K 2, L 8, M 18, N 32.
- Electron binding energy is the energy required to remove an electron from an atom.
- It is higher for inner shells and higher for a higher atomic number: tungsten K about 69.5 keV, oxygen K about 0.5 keV.

# Ionisation and excitation
id: ionisation
summary: What happens when an electron gains energy, and why ionisation matters in the body.
areas: Ionisation and excitation
diagram: bohr
status: draft
sources: 2-2 s15–20

An electron can be given energy by a passing photon or electron. What happens next depends on how much energy it is given.

In **ionisation**, the electron absorbs sufficient energy to overcome its binding energy and escapes from its shell. The atom is no longer electrically neutral: it has a positive charge and is a **positive ion**. The freed electron and the positive ion together are an **ion pair**.

In **excitation**, the electron is given less energy. It may move further away from the nucleus without being freed from the atom. The excited electron then returns to the ground state, releasing the excess energy as a photon. The photon's energy is equal to the difference between the excited state and the ground state.

Ionisation matters in the body. In tissue it breaks chemical bonds and can damage **DNA**, directly or through free radicals formed from water. This is the source of radiation risk, and the reason every exposure must be justified and optimised.

## Try this
- Try **Excitation**, then **Ionisation**. Only ionisation leaves a charged atom, because only then does the electron escape.

## Key points
- Ionisation: the electron absorbs sufficient energy to overcome its binding energy and escapes, leaving a positive ion.
- Excitation: the electron is given less energy, moves further from the nucleus and stays in the atom, then returns to the ground state.
- In the body, ionisation can damage DNA: the source of radiation risk.

# Characteristic radiation
id: characteristic
summary: Why an electron dropping into an inner shell gives a photon of a fixed energy.
areas: Characteristic radiation
diagram: bohr
status: draft
sources: 2-2 s18–20; 8-1 s18

When an inner-shell electron is ejected, it leaves a **vacancy**. The vacancy is filled by an electron from an outer shell. A photon of electromagnetic radiation is then emitted, with an energy equivalent to the **difference in binding energies** between the shells involved.

In tungsten, an **L** electron filling a **K** vacancy gives 69.5 − 10.2 ≈ **59.3 keV** (Kα). An **M** electron filling it gives about **67.2 keV** (Kβ).

This is **characteristic radiation**. Each element has its own binding energies, so the photon energies are characteristic of the element. Tungsten's K-lines are always at about 59 and 67 keV, whatever the tube voltage. Topic 3 shows how they appear as lines on the X-ray spectrum.

## Try this
- Choose tungsten and step through ionisation of the K shell: watch the L electron fill the vacancy and the photon leave.
- Compare its energy with the K-lines on the spectrum diagram in topic 3.

## Key points
- An electron from an outer shell fills an inner-shell vacancy, and a photon is emitted with the difference in binding energies.
- Tungsten: L to K gives Kα, about 59.3 keV; M to K gives Kβ, about 67.2 keV.
- The energies are fixed for each element: they are characteristic of it.

# The electromagnetic spectrum
id: em-spectrum
summary: Where X-rays sit among other electromagnetic radiation, and what makes them different.
areas: EM spectrum
diagram: em-spectrum
status: draft
sources: 3-2 s27–32

X-rays are one part of the **electromagnetic (EM) spectrum**. From the longest wavelength to the shortest:

- **Radio waves** and **microwaves** have the longest wavelengths and the lowest energies.
- **Infrared** has wavelengths from about 1 mm down to 700 nm. It is felt as heat.
- **Visible** light runs from 700 nm (red light) to 400 nm (violet light). It is visible to the human eye, and is a very small part of the whole spectrum.
- **Ultraviolet** has wavelengths from 400 nm to 10 nm. At its shorter wavelengths the frequency and energy are high enough to cause cellular damage.
- **X-rays** have wavelengths from 10 nm to 0.001 nm. They have enough energy to penetrate deep into tissues and cause ionising damage to cells.
- **Gamma rays** carry the most energy and have the shortest wavelengths, less than 10⁻¹² m.

As the wavelength gets shorter, the **frequency** and the photon **energy** rise. All EM radiation travels at the **speed of light** in a vacuum, about 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

**Ionising radiation** begins at the upper limit of ultraviolet and includes X-rays and gamma rays. X-rays and gamma rays overlap in energy, and both are generally described by their energy. They differ in **where they come from**: X-rays from electrons outside the nucleus, and gamma rays from the nucleus, in nuclear reactions and radioactive decay.

EM radiation is not just a continuous wave. It also behaves as separate packets of energy called **photons**. This is **wave–particle duality**. In radiography we usually think in photons.

X-rays also travel in straight lines, carry no charge (so they cannot be focused or deflected), are invisible, penetrate matter by different amounts depending on the tissue, and cause some materials to fluoresce.

## Key points
- In order of energy: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
- A shorter wavelength means a higher frequency and a higher energy; all travel at the speed of light.
- Ionising radiation: the upper limit of ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays.
- X-rays come from electrons, gamma rays from the nucleus.
- X-rays are invisible, carry no charge, travel in straight lines and penetrate tissue by different amounts.
