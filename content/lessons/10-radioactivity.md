# The nucleus and stability
id: nucleus
summary: Atomic and mass numbers, isotopes, and why some nuclei are unstable.
areas: Nucleus and isotopes; Nuclear stability
diagram: nuclides
status: draft
sources: 12-1 s2–17

**Radioactivity** is the act of emitting radiation spontaneously. Radiation is emitted so that the atomic nucleus can become stable.

The central nucleus contains **protons** and **neutrons**. A proton is far more massive than an electron, and has the same size of charge, but positive. A neutron has a similar mass to a proton and no charge.

Each element is determined by the number of protons in its nucleus: the **atomic number**, **Z**. In a neutral atom this is matched by the number of orbital electrons. The **atomic mass number**, **A**, is the number of protons plus neutrons. Hydrogen is the simplest nucleus, with one proton. Helium has two protons, which repel each other, so it needs stabilising material: neutrons act as the "glue". With 2 protons and 2 neutrons, helium has Z = 2 and A = 4.

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. They have the same number of protons (Z) but different mass numbers (A). Carbon always has an atomic number of 6, with a varying number of neutrons: carbon-12 (nearly 99%) and carbon-13 (nearly 1%) are stable, while carbon-14 is unstable.

There are opposing forces in the nucleus:

- **Electrostatic repulsion** between the protons acts to disrupt the nucleus.
- **Short-range attractive forces** act to bind the nucleus together.

As Z increases, more neutrons are required for stability. A nucleus is **unstable** if the disrupting forces are greater than the binding forces. An unstable nucleus is radioactive, and is termed a **radionuclide**: it acts to change to a more stable form. Its excess energy can be emitted from the nucleus as gamma radiation, transferred to one of its electrons, or used to create and emit a new particle (an alpha or beta particle).

## Try this
- Compare **carbon-12** and **carbon-14**: the same element, but one is off the band of stability.
- Predict **iodine-131** and **iodine-123**: why do two isotopes of one element decay differently?
- Choose **radium-226**: what happens beyond lead?

## Key points
- Z = the number of protons, which determines the element. A = protons + neutrons.
- Isotopes: the same number of protons (Z), different mass numbers (A).
- Electrostatic repulsion disrupts the nucleus; short-range attractive forces bind it. As Z increases, more neutrons are required for stability.
- An unstable nucleus emits radiation to change to a more stable form: radioactivity.

# Activity and half-life
id: half-life
summary: Becquerels, random disintegration, and the exponential fall in activity.
areas: Activity and half-life
diagram: halflife
status: draft
sources: 12-1 s17, s21–25

Most nuclei are stable. Radioactivity is the result of an unstable nucleus acting to achieve stability, and each reorganisation is a **disintegration**.

**Activity** is the number of disintegrations each second. One disintegration per second is an activity of 1 **becquerel** (Bq), the SI unit of radioactivity. In practice activity is given in **megabecquerels** (MBq): 1 MBq = 1,000,000 Bq.

Disintegrations are **random**: it is not possible to predict when one particular nucleus will disintegrate. With large numbers of nuclei, however, probability can be applied to predict the disintegration rate, and the decay rate is characteristic of each radionuclide.

The **half-life** is the time taken for **half** of the material to decay. It is an **exponential** process: the same fraction decays in each half-life. The fraction of material remaining is (½)ⁿ, where n is the number of elapsed half-lives: after one half-life 50% remains, after two 25%, after three 12.5%. Technetium-99m has a half-life of about **6 hours**.

## Predict
After three half-lives, what fraction of the radioactive nuclei remains?
- A third
- * An eighth
- None
> The fraction remaining is (½)ⁿ: a half, a quarter, then an eighth.

## Try this
- Step one half-life at a time: 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%.
- Change to 36 nuclei: the sample moves off the expected curve, because disintegrations are random.
- Change the radionuclide: the shape stays the same; only the time scale changes.

## Key points
- Activity: disintegrations per second, in becquerels (Bq).
- Each disintegration is random; with large numbers of nuclei the rate can be predicted.
- Half-life: the time taken for half of the material to decay.
- Decay is exponential: the fraction remaining is (½)ⁿ after n half-lives.

# Alpha decay
id: alpha
summary: What an alpha particle is, and why alpha emitters are unsuitable for radiology.
areas: Alpha decay
diagram: decay
status: draft
sources: 12-1 s27–29

A radioactive disintegration may change the atomic number Z (and hence the element), the atomic mass A, or both, depending on the decay process: alpha, beta or gamma.

**Alpha decay** is a common process. It occurs when the nucleus is too big: the electrostatic forces disrupt the nucleus, the binding forces are overcome, and an alpha particle is ejected.

An **alpha particle** is 2 protons and 2 neutrons: a **helium nucleus**. When it is emitted, the nucleus loses two protons and two neutrons, so the atomic mass number **decreases by 4** and the atomic number **decreases by 2**. A new element is formed, two places lower in the periodic table. Radium-226, for example, becomes radon-222.

The properties of the alpha particle:

- heavy (4 atomic mass units), with a positive charge of 2;
- strongly ionising, but with a very short range;
- easily stopped, for example by thin plastic.

Alpha emitters are **unsuitable for radiology**. The particles are difficult to detect outside the body, and their short range in tissue gives a heavy local dose.

- * Z decreases by 2; A decreases by 4
- Z increases by 1; A is unchanged
> An alpha particle is 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so the nucleus loses 2 from Z and 4 from A.

## Key points
- An alpha particle is a helium nucleus: 2 protons and 2 neutrons, with a charge of +2.
- Alpha decay: Z decreases by 2, A decreases by 4, and a new element is formed.
- Strongly ionising with a very short range: unsuitable for radiology.

# Beta decay
id: beta
summary: Beta-minus, beta-plus, annihilation and electron capture.
areas: Beta decay
diagram: decay
status: draft
sources: 12-1 s30–35

In beta decay the nucleons themselves transform.

**Beta-minus (β⁻) decay** occurs in a nucleus with **too many neutrons**. A **neutron becomes a proton**, with the emission of a high-energy, high-speed **electron** (the β⁻ particle) and an **antineutrino**. A remains the same and **Z increases by 1**, so it is a different element.

**Beta-plus (β⁺) decay** occurs in a nucleus with **too many protons**. A **proton becomes a neutron**, with the emission of a **positron** and a **neutrino**. A positron is exactly the same as an electron but with a positive charge. A remains the same and **Z decreases by 1**.

When the positron meets an electron, both are **annihilated**: their mass becomes energy, as **two 511 keV photons** travelling in opposite directions. This is the basis of PET imaging. (Slide 35 differs: the reference physics is two photons.)

**Electron capture** is the other process for a nucleus with too many protons. An **inner orbital electron** is pulled into the nucleus, a proton becomes a neutron, and a **neutrino** is emitted. **Characteristic X-rays** follow as the vacancy in the shell is filled. (Slide 34 gives a gamma ray; the reference physics is a neutrino and characteristic X-rays.)

Beta particles are less ionising than alpha particles. They have a range of several centimetres in air and are stopped by a few millimetres of plastic or aluminium. They are completely absorbed in tissue, giving a large local dose, so they are not suitable for imaging.

## Try this
- Build the daughter nucleus for beta-minus, beta-plus and electron capture, and compare how Z changes.

## Key points
- Beta-minus: a neutron becomes a proton; an electron and an antineutrino are emitted; Z increases by 1, A remains the same.
- Beta-plus: a proton becomes a neutron; a positron and a neutrino are emitted; Z decreases by 1, A remains the same.
- Annihilation: a positron and an electron give two 511 keV photons in opposite directions.
- Electron capture: an inner electron is pulled into the nucleus; a neutrino is emitted, then characteristic X-rays.

# Gamma rays and technetium-99m
id: gamma-tc99m
summary: Where gamma rays come from, and why technetium-99m is so useful for imaging.
areas: Gamma and technetium-99m
diagram: decay
status: draft
sources: 12-1 s36–40

**Gamma rays** are part of the electromagnetic spectrum. They have the same properties as X-rays: they are ionising, they penetrate matter in proportion to their energy, and they are not influenced by electric or magnetic fields. They **differ in their production**: gamma rays come from the nucleus, X-rays from electrons outside it. Gamma emission does not change Z or A; it lowers the energy of an excited nucleus.

Gamma emission is usually rapid after alpha or beta decay. But the emission can be delayed: the daughter nucleus remains excited for a measurable time. While it is in the excited state it is called **metastable** (the "m").

This is very important in radiology, because of **technetium-99m**. Molybdenum-99 decays by beta-minus emission to technetium-99m, which then decays to technetium-99 with a half-life of **6 hours**, emitting a **140 keV gamma ray**:

- Mo-99 → Tc-99m + β⁻
- Tc-99m → Tc-99 + γ

Separate the technetium-99m from the molybdenum-99 and we have a **pure gamma emitter**, used as the radioisotope in radionuclide imaging. Gamma rays are useful for radiology because they are easily detected and penetrating, which means less dose:

- A pure gamma emitter gives no local dose from alpha or beta particles.
- A **140 keV** gamma ray penetrates the body and is easily detected by a gamma camera.
- A **6-hour half-life** is long enough to prepare and image, and short enough to keep the dose low.

## Try this
- Choose **Gamma, from a metastable state**: Z and A remain the same.

## Key points
- Gamma rays have the same properties as X-rays; they differ in their production. Gamma rays come from the nucleus.
- Gamma emission lowers the energy of the nucleus without changing Z or A.
- Tc-99m: metastable; produced from Mo-99; 6-hour half-life; 140 keV gamma ray; a pure gamma emitter.
